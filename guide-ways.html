Ways to handle…

Common moments, and a few ways to meet each without a bad month. Each way works; pick the one that fits how you live. The figures are examples, not suggestions.

A trip

A trip is a big cost you can usually see coming. Three ways to pay for it:

Save up for it

Suits you if you know roughly what it costs and when you go.

  1. On the computer, on Budget, point at the Travel group and choose + Add a category, for example Beach trip.
  2. Work out the monthly amount: $2,850 over six months is $475.
  3. Place that each month. A monthly target on the category reminds you what it needs.

When you go, the money is there. On the computer, Placed vs Spent marks that month Paid from savings, not over: you spent more than you placed that month, and the money saved earlier paid for it. Afterwards, open the category and choose Retire: it leaves Budget, and anything left in it goes back to Ready to place.

One pot for all trips

Suits you if you travel often and plans move around.

  1. Place a steady amount into Travel each month, like $300.
  2. Give each trip its own category under Travel, so you can still see what it cost.
  3. Every trip draws on the same money.

What you don't spend stays there for the next trip. Nothing to finish.

Pay it that month

Suits you if it's a short trip and the money is there now. In the month you go, place the trip's cost into Travel and spend from it as usual. Simple, though that month carries the whole cost.

Which one?One big trip with a date: save up for it. Several trips a year: one pot. A weekend away with the money already there: pay it that month.

Yearly insurance

A bill you know is coming, once a year. Split it into twelve: a $1,440 premium is $120 a month. Give it its own category with a monthly target of $120 and place that each month. When the bill arrives, the category holds it, and that month looks like any other. The same works for road tax, a yearly subscription, or anything else that comes round once a year.

A new phone or laptop

Saving up for one big buy. Give it a category, decide when you'd like to buy, and divide: $1,800 in nine months is $200 a month. Savings goals & limits on Budget can follow it with an amount and a date, and shows how close you are. If the old one breaks early, move what's missing in from a category that can spare it, and the plan simply ends sooner.

Pay that changes each month

Commission, freelance or shift work: you don't know next month's pay. Place only money that has already come in. Ready to place shows exactly that: money that's arrived and has no job yet. Fill the basics first (rent, food, bills), then the rest as more comes in. Once you're a month ahead, you can place this month's spending from last month's pay, and a small month stops being a problem.

A bonus

A big lump of money arriving at once. It comes in as income, so it shows in Ready to place, waiting for a job. There's no rush to place it all on the day. Some people top up what's short, some put a part towards a bigger plan (a trip, a goal, a debt), and some spread it across the coming months. Whatever you choose, give each dollar a job, and it won't quietly disappear into everyday spending.

A card you can't clear yet

Carrying a balance on a card is common, and nothing to hide from the app. When you start, if the cards owe more than the bank holds, Ready to place starts at $0 and the rest stays owed on the cards. Then, on the computer, open Nest › Debts and choose Edit on the card (or Add a payoff plan, once it's carrying a balance past its bill): type its rate and what you'll pay each month, and the planner shows when it's paid off, and how much interest a little extra each month saves. A card left owing after its bill also says Carried since the month it started, so you can watch it shrink.
